Design and build

Permaculture design, built by a local landscaping crew

Most permaculture designers draw a plan and hand it to you. Terramorph does both halves: we design the property, then our crews install it, from grading and drainage to planting and mulch, and we come back to check how it is settling in. You get one team that understands the plan because they wrote it.

Permaculture isn't a style or a plant list. It's a way of designing. The word was coined in Tasmania in the mid-1970s by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren, and it has grown into a set of design principles used on farms, schools and backyards around the world. The goal is simple: a landscape that works like a natural system, so it keeps producing with less input from you.

02

What it means

What permaculture means for a Toledo-area yard

In plain English, a permaculture design answers five questions about your property. Good design starts with watching the site before building anything: where the water goes after a storm, which way the wind comes from, and where the sun falls in June versus December.

  1. WaterWhere does rain land, where does it run, and how do we slow it down and soak it in instead of sending it to the street?
  2. SoilWhat is the soil doing now, and how do we build it up instead of stripping it every year?
  3. ZonesWhat do you use every day, and what can be left a little wilder? Permaculture places things by how often you visit them, from the kitchen herbs by the back door (Zone 1) to vegetable beds (Zone 2), fruit trees and berry shrubs (Zone 3), and a quiet wild corner for wildlife (Zone 5).
  4. PlantsWhich plants can do more than one job? A fruit tree "guild" surrounds a tree with companions that each help: clover to add nitrogen, flowers to bring in bees, bulbs to hold back grass.
  5. HabitatHow do we make room for the bees, birds and beneficial insects that do a lot of the pest control for free?
03

Built for here

Designing for Northwest Ohio: clay, wet springs and zone 6b

Permaculture advice online is often written for somewhere else. Northwest Ohio has its own rules, and we design for the soil you actually have. See best plants for Ohio clay soil.

  1. Heavy clayMuch of Wood and Lucas County sits on the old Great Black Swamp. Clay soils like the Hoytville series, which was first described right here in Wood County, drain very slowly. That changes how we handle water and which plants we choose.
  2. Wet springs, dry AugustsYards here often sit soggy in April and bake by midsummer, so designs have to handle both.
  3. Zone 6b wintersPerrysburg and Toledo are in USDA hardiness zone 6b on the 2023 map, so plants have to handle average winter lows of -5 to 0 °F.
  4. Sand pocketsWest of Toledo, the Oak Openings region is sandy and drains fast. Plants that thrive there can fail on clay a few miles away.

Water first

Water first: rain gardens, swales and drainage on clay

Permaculture is famous for swales, shallow ditches on contour that slow water so it soaks in. On our clay, a swale dug without testing can just make a long puddle. So we test first. We run a simple percolation test (dig a hole, fill it, check how fast it drains), then pick the right tool.

Rain gardens stay at least 10 feet from foundations and away from septic systems, and every dig starts with an OHIO811 utility locate.

  1. Rain gardensFor roof and driveway runoff: shallow planted basins that soak water in within a few days. On clay we make them shallower and mix 2 to 6 inches of compost into the top layer.
  2. Swales and bermsOnly where the soil test says water will actually soak in.
  3. Conventional drainageWhere it's the honest answer. Sometimes a French drain or regrading is the right first step. See drainage solutions.
  4. Rainwater harvestingOne inch of rain on 1,000 square feet of roof is about 623 gallons, and the design decides where that water goes.

Small lots

Front yards, small lots and neighborhoods with rules

Permaculture works on a small suburban lot, not just acreage. The key in town is making it look intentional: clean bed edges, a mown path or border, plants grouped in drifts, and a clear front-yard design so it reads as a garden, not neglect. We keep lawn where it has a job (play, pets, paths) and plant the rest. See native plants vs. lawn.

07

Why it matters

The values behind it

Permaculture is built on three simple ethics: care for the earth, care for people, and share the surplus. For a homeowner that means a yard that costs less to keep up, gives back food and habitat, and does its part for local water. In our area that matters: runoff carrying phosphorus into the Maumee River feeds Lake Erie's summer algae blooms, the same blooms that shut down Toledo's tap water in August 2014. Yards that soak in rain and skip excess fertilizer are a small part of the fix.

Questions homeowners ask

Quick answers before you request an estimate.

What is permaculture design?

Permaculture is a way of designing a landscape so it works like a natural system: it catches and stores water, builds soil, mixes plants that help each other, and makes room for wildlife. Unlike a traditional yard of lawn and shrubs, it is designed to produce food, habitat or both, with less mowing and watering over time.

What does a permaculture designer do?

A permaculture designer studies your property first (water flow, soil, sun, wind and how you use the space) and then lays out zones, water features, plantings and paths that work together. Terramorph also installs the design with our own crews.

How much does a permaculture design cost?

It depends on the size of the property and how much you want installed at once. Terramorph starts with a free estimate, then prices the design and the installation phases separately so you can build in stages.

Does permaculture work in Ohio's clay soil and zone 6b winters?

Yes, if it's designed for them. On Northwest Ohio clay we test drainage before digging swales, use rain gardens with compost-amended soil, and choose plants proven in zone 6b and heavy soil.

Can I do permaculture on a small yard or a front yard?

Yes. Small lots often get the most out of it: a rain garden for the downspout, a few fruit trees with companion plants, native beds and a kitchen garden. In front yards we keep clean edges and a clear layout so it looks designed and fits neighborhood expectations.

Is permaculture the same as a food forest?

No. A food forest is one thing a permaculture design can include: a layered planting of fruit and nut trees, shrubs and ground covers. Permaculture is the whole-property plan, including water, soil, habitat and how you live in the space.

Do you install the design or just draw it?

Both. Terramorph designs the property and our crews install it, from grading and drainage to planting, mulch and paths, then we follow up while it establishes.

How long until a permaculture yard is productive and low-maintenance?

Water features and beds work right away. Perennials fill in over the first two to three growing seasons, and fruit and nut trees take several years to bear. Weeding and watering are heaviest in the first seasons and drop as plants close in.

Is permaculture sustainable and regenerative?

That's the goal: a permaculture yard holds rainwater on site, builds soil with compost and mulch, supports pollinators and birds, and cuts down on mowing, watering and fertilizer.
