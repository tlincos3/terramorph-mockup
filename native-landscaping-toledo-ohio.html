Designed, not abandoned

Native gardens that look designed, not abandoned

The biggest worry we hear about native plants is "will it look messy?" It does not have to. A native garden reads as intentional when it has clean bed edges, a mown border or path, plants grouped in drifts instead of scattered singles, and a plan for every season. That is the difference between a designed native landscape and a vacant lot, and it is the part we specialize in.

Why it matters here

Why native plants matter here

Native plants aren't just a look. They're the base of the local food web. North America has about 2.9 billion fewer birds than in 1970. A yard won't fix that alone, but a neighborhood of native yards adds real habitat back.

  1. Caterpillars feed birdsRaising one brood of chickadees takes an estimated 6,000 to 9,000 caterpillars, and most caterpillars can only eat the native plants they evolved with.
  2. A few plants do the heavy liftingNative oaks support the caterpillars of 557 species of moths and butterflies, and black cherry 456. Non-native ginkgo and zelkova support none. Research shows just 14% of native plant groups support more than 90% of caterpillar species, so choosing the right "keystone" plants matters more than planting a lot.
  3. Yards can make or break a bird populationIn a study of suburban yards, chickadees sustained their population only where more than 70% of the plants were native.
  4. Ohio's bees need groundOhio has an estimated 400 to 450 bee species. Most native bees live alone rather than in hives, and about 70% of them nest in bare, well-drained soil, so good habitat leaves some sunny ground open.

Built for here

Built for Northwest Ohio: Black Swamp clay, Oak Openings sand and zone 6b

Two very different native palettes live within a few miles of each other here. We plan for the soil you actually have and for zone 6b winters (USDA 2023 map). See best plants for Ohio clay soil.

Deep roots help on clay too: native prairie grasses like switchgrass can reach 8 to 11 feet deep and big bluestem 6 to 7 feet, opening paths for water that lawn grass never reaches.

  1. Clay countryMost of Wood and Lucas County, the old Great Black Swamp: plants that handle slow drainage and wet springs, like swamp milkweed ("a good plant for wet, even mucky, clay soil if there is sufficient sun," per OSU Extension) and switchgrass, which tolerates poorly drained soil and occasional flooding.
  2. Oak Openings sandWest of Toledo: fast-draining sand over clay, home to about a third of Ohio's state-listed rare plants and animals. Plants like wild lupine thrive there but hate clay.

Rain gardens

Rain gardens for wet yards and downspouts

A rain garden is a shallow, planted basin that catches runoff from a roof or driveway and lets it soak in, usually within a few days. It isn't a pond. Done right on clay, it means a few things.

Rain gardens are one drainage tool. When a yard needs regrading or a French drain instead, we'll tell you. See drainage solutions.

  1. A percolation test firstWe check how fast your soil actually absorbs water before sizing the basin.
  2. A shallower basin on clayWith 2 to 6 inches of compost mixed into the soil so water soaks in.
  3. Placed safelyAt least 10 feet from the foundation, away from septic systems, and not on steep slopes.
  4. The right plantsPlants that handle both standing water and summer drought.
06

Invasives

Invasives: what to remove first

Ohio bans the sale and propagation of 63 invasive plants, including bush honeysuckle, common buckthorn, garlic mustard, autumn olive, tree-of-heaven and Callery pear (banned from sale since January 2023). Bush honeysuckle is the big one locally: it leafs out earlier and holds its leaves later than native shrubs, shading out wildflowers and young trees, and its berries lack the fat migrating birds need. It's best cut in early summer, and treated stumps need watching for at least a year. You're not required to remove existing plants, but replacing them with natives is one of the biggest habitat upgrades a yard can get.

Questions homeowners ask

Quick answers before you request an estimate.

Still wondering about something? Call 419-873-6801, 7 a.m. to 8 p.m.

How much does native landscaping cost?

It depends on the size of the area, site prep (removing lawn or invasives, soil and drainage work), plant sizes and whether you want an establishment-care plan. Terramorph gives free estimates and can phase larger projects over several seasons.

Are native gardens really low maintenance?

Lower, not zero. The first one to two seasons need watering and weeding while roots establish. By the third season most beds fill in, weeding drops and they need far less water, mowing and fertilizer than lawn.

Will a native garden look messy or get me a weed notice?

Not when it's designed. Clean bed edges, mown borders or paths, plants grouped in drifts and a clear layout make native gardens read as intentional. We design front-yard native beds with neighbors and city expectations in mind.

How big should a rain garden be, and how deep?

It depends on the roof or driveway area draining to it and how fast your soil absorbs water. On Northwest Ohio clay we run a percolation test, keep the basin shallower, and mix compost into the top few inches so water soaks in within a few days.

Do rain gardens attract mosquitoes?

A properly built rain garden drains within a few days, and mosquitoes need standing water for about a week to develop from egg to adult. If water stands longer than that, the design needs adjusting, and we check that during establishment.

Can you convert part of my lawn to a meadow or native garden?

Yes. Most properties do best with a mix: lawn where you play and walk, and native beds or meadow in low-use areas, edges and wet spots. We remove the turf, prepare the soil, plant, and leave clean edges and paths.

Which plants are native to Northwest Ohio?

It depends on your soil. Clay sites suit plants like swamp milkweed, switchgrass, coneflowers and native asters. The sandy Oak Openings area west of Toledo has its own palette, like wild lupine, which won't grow in clay. We match plants to your site.

When is the best time to plant a native or pollinator garden in Ohio?

Spring and fall are both good. Fall planting lets roots grow in cool, moist soil, and many native seeds need a winter to sprout. Spring planting works well for potted plants with good follow-up watering.

Can you remove invasive honeysuckle and buckthorn?

Yes. We cut and treat invasive shrubs like bush honeysuckle and buckthorn, haul them away, check for resprouts, and replant with native shrubs so the area doesn't fill back in with invasives.

Do you work with clay soil and wet yards?

Yes. Most of our area is heavy clay from the old Great Black Swamp. We choose plants that tolerate wet clay, use rain gardens where they fit, and handle regrading or drainage when that's the real fix.
